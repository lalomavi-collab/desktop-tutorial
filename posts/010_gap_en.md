Nobody gets sued for using artificial intelligence.

They get sued for something else entirely: that when the output was wrong, nobody knew who was answerable for it.

That sounds like a semantic distinction. It is not. It is the difference between a tool and an exposure.

When a lawyer relies on a junior associate, the chain of responsibility is obvious. The associate erred, the lawyer signed, the lawyer answers for it. Everyone knows where they stand.

When an organization relies on a language model, that chain does not form on its own. It has to be built: who checked, what was checked, on what basis the call was made, and who signed at the end. Most organizations I meet never built it. Not out of negligence. Nobody told them it was needed.

The gap is invisible on the day you adopt the tool.

It becomes visible on the day someone starts asking questions: in a committee, in due diligence, in court.
