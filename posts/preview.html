Nobody gets sued for using artificial intelligence. They get sued for something else entirely: that when the output was wrong, nobody knew who was answerable for it. That sounds like a semantic distinction. It is not. It is the difference between a tool and an exposure. When a lawyer relies on a junior associate, the chain of responsibility is obvious. The associate erred, the lawyer signed, the lawyer answers for it. Everyone knows where they stand. When an organization relies on a language model, that chain does not form on its own. It has to be built: who checked, what was checked, on what basis the call was made, and who signed at the end. Most organizations I meet never built it. Not out of negligence. Nobody told them it was needed. The gap is invisible on the day you adopt the tool. It becomes visible on the day someone starts asking questions: in a committee, in due diligence, in court. Your legal exposure to AI is not in the model. That is the most common mistake I meet. Executives audit the tool. Which model, where the data sits, is it encrypted. Good questions, all of them covering the one place where the damage almost never happens. The damage happens in three much quieter places. **One: the data that went in.** Who put it there, where it came from, and whether there was permission to use it. A client contract pasted into a public model to "summarize this quickly" is not a shortcut. It is an incident. **Two: the decision nobody documented.** The system recommended, someone accepted, nobody recorded the basis. A year later, asked how the decision was reached, there is no answer. Missing documentation is not a technicality. In a legal proceeding it is often the whole case. **Three: the vendor contract.** Almost every AI licensing agreement moves responsibility for the output onto you. It is written there, quietly, in a clause nobody read. You signed, you own it. All three look entirely sound on the day you sign. All three detonate on the day someone starts asking questions. "We are an Israeli company, the EU AI Act does not apply to us." I hear that sentence often, and in most cases it is not accurate. The European regulation does not ask where the company is registered. It asks where the users are, and where the output of the system is used. If you have customers in Europe, or your product serves users there, the question is already on your desk. And it is not one question. It is a chain: Is your system classified as high risk? Who is the provider and who is the deployer, legally rather than commercially? Is there a documented risk management system? Who signs the declaration of conformity? Most organizations I meet cannot answer the first one, which is why they never reached the other three. This is not an emergency. It is also not next year's problem. Classification is a few weeks of work when done in order, and considerably more when done under pressure from a customer demanding an answer or from a due diligence process. The only difference between the two is when you start.
